Clinical trials

Do you do clinical trial recruitment across the UK?

Yes. 15,000 people in the UK living with long-term conditions have joined us (November 2026, and growing daily), built from the communities clinical trials miss and profiled to postcode district, so before you start we can tell you how many members near your sites match and have already said yes. We work with sponsors, CROs with UK sites and academic clinical trials units. Members who pass your screening questions and say yes are referred through the site's own referral link, and we follow every referral through to randomisation.

Can a sponsor based outside the UK work with you?

Yes. Our members are in the UK; the sponsor, biotech or CRO can be anywhere. We tell you the same working day how many members near your UK sites match and have already said yes, test your materials with members before the ethics submission, and refer members who pass your screening questions and say yes. Sponsors never learn who members are. More for sponsors outside the UK.

How do you check patient recruitment feasibility in the UK?

With free patient feasibility, the same working day. Site feasibility asks the sites to estimate how many patients they could find; we ask the patients themselves. Tell us the condition, the people you need and where your UK sites are, and we tell you how many members near each site match, by postcode district, how many have already opted in to hear about clinical trials, and how they break down by age, sex, ethnicity and deprivation. They're pre-screened, never eligible: real people who've said yes, not a forecast of enrolment.

How long does it take to set up a clinical trial in the UK?

The UK government's target was to get clinical trials up and running within 150 days by March 2026. With data to March 2026, the average set-up time for a commercial interventional clinical trial was 122 days, down from 169 days. Ethics and regulatory approval come through one application, combined review, and 99% of combined review applications received regulatory approval within the 60-day target.

Source: HRA, UK clinical trials reach 150 day set-up milestone, April 2026.

What changed in the UK clinical trials regulations in April 2026?

The updated regulations came into force on 28 April 2026. Combined review, one application for ethics and regulatory approval, is now law, and there's a streamlined notification scheme for some applications and modifications. Clinical trials involving medicines must be registered in a public registry. Sponsors must publish a results summary within 12 months of the end and offer it to participants in a way they can easily understand. A clinical trial is expected to recruit its first UK participant within two years of approval, or the approval lapses unless an extension is granted.

Source: HRA, Transforming the UK's clinical trial landscape.

How does patient recruitment work?

For UK clinical trials, members who pass your screening questions and say yes are referred through the site's own referral link, with the yes recorded and a best time to call. Eligibility stays the site's call: conditions are self-reported, with qualifying questions. We then follow each one from the referral to randomisation, across seven steps: Referred, Contacted, Booked, Attended, Consented, Screened, Randomised, with screen failures reported with the question that failed, and randomisation checked against your own record. You get one line per patient each week: where the patient says things stand, the site's note beside it, and why anyone stopped. Sponsors never learn who members are: only we, and the site a member says yes to, know their name.

What happens if the site doesn't call?

We tell the site first. Anyone the site hasn't reached by day 7 goes back to the site that week, so the site hears first and can add its own note. If they're still not reached a week later, they go into the sponsor's report with the site's note beside them. It's a record of where each patient is, never a score for the site.

What do members agree to before they're referred?

Two consents before any detail leaves us: a standing opt-in to hear about clinical trials, then a yes to one named clinical trial. The site's informed consent is a third step, and never ours.

Are members paid to join a clinical trial?

No. Members are paid for their time when a client funds the work, at £27.50 an hour, in line with NIHR's rate for public involvement. Nobody is paid for joining us or for joining a clinical trial, and the check-ins after a referral are unpaid.

How are your recruitment fees structured?

Fees are tied to agreed deliverables. Fees per randomised participant are checked against the site's own record and capped, with a credit or refund if we fall short of the referrals we commit to. The arrangement sits in the contract and in the recruitment arrangements the ethics committee sees; eligibility, consent and randomisation stay the site's. The first clinical trial with a new client runs as a fixed-price pilot agreed jointly, with a referral commitment and a credit for every referral short of it.

The condition is self-reported. How do I know they have it?

They're pre-screened, never eligible. We screen on the questions we agree with you, and your site confirms diagnosis. Every screen failure comes back to us with the question that failed, so the screener gets tighter.

Can you help with an HRA Inclusion and Diversity Plan?

Yes. Patient feasibility answers questions 1, 3 and 4 with real people near your sites: who is affected by the condition, what your recruitment goals are, and how you'll recruit and retain them. Submitting a plan is not mandatory, but the HRA and MHRA encourage it, and from early 2027 they will ask more researchers and sponsors to volunteer one alongside their applications.

Sources: HRA, Inclusion and Diversity Plan guidance, updated 2 November 2026; MHRA, pilot findings, 3 November 2026.

Does following patients up improve enrolment?

We don't claim it does: we haven't found controlled evidence that following patients up raises enrolment. It gives the team the facts, where each referral stands and why anyone stopped, not a promised result.

How is this different from Be Part of Research?

Be Part of Research is NIHR's UK volunteer register, free for research funded or supported by the NIHR. On it, research teams report how many volunteers were interested and how many took part. Use both. The register finds people after approval and tells you little about why they'll say yes or no. We add the patient's own account of where things stand, with the site's note beside it, and the same members can review the protocol and the invitation before the protocol is final.

Patient engagement and PPI

What is patient and public involvement (PPI)?

Patient and public involvement means research is done with or by patients and the public, not to, about or for them. We call our version patient engagement; UK academic research and ethics committees call it PPI. In practice, people who live with a condition help shape the question, the design and the materials before a clinical trial or product goes ahead. It's different from taking part as a participant.

Source: NIHR, briefing notes for researchers on public involvement.

Is a survey of members involvement or consultation?

A survey or an on-screen review of a finished document is consultation: people are asked, and the client decides. Involvement in the sense the UK Standards for Public Involvement mean is people helping to shape the research from the question onward, as named contributors. We do both. Surveys, reviews and the report are consultation at scale, and we call them that. The advisory add-on, where members join your steering or advisory group as named, paid contributors for the life of the research, is involvement. A funding committee or an ethics committee will still expect to see how patients shaped the question and the design, and a report on one finished document doesn't replace that.

What's the difference between patient engagement and taking part in a clinical trial?

Patient engagement, what NIHR calls involvement, means helping to shape the clinical trial. Taking part means being in it. Our members do both, separately: they help shape plans, products and materials, and members who pass a clinical trial's screening questions and say yes can be referred to take part. They're never paid to join a clinical trial.

How do I answer the IRAS public involvement questions?

Say who you involved, what they did, and what changed because of it. The IRAS form asks how many people you involved, what they did, and how it made your clinical trial more acceptable to the people taking part. A strong answer describes the people involved by their characteristics, lists what they raised, and says what you changed and what you didn't. Our report is written to give you exactly that.

Do patients need to review the participant information sheet before the ethics submission?

It's expected. The Health Research Authority's checklist for applicants says: "Participant information developed without public involvement will not normally receive a favourable opinion from REC." Put the information sheet, consent form and invitation in front of people who have the condition before you submit.

Source: HRA, checklist for applicants on public involvement and ethical review.

How much should you pay patients for involvement in research?

In the UK, NIHR's recommended rate for public involvement is £27.50 for an activity of about an hour or less that needs little or no preparation, after NIHR raised its rates by 10% in December 2025. Its rates run from £13.80 to £495: see every rate and what it covers. We pay members £27.50 an hour, in line with NIHR's rate for public involvement, for work a client funds, worked out for the time each task takes, and we publish our full rate card.

Source: NIHR payment rates for public involvement, updated December 2025.

How many patients should you involve?

There's no fixed number; it depends on the question. To see whether a point comes from one group or from everyone, you need enough people in each group. We typically ask 150 to 300 members in surveys and around 40 for detailed on-screen reviews.

How are your members different from a patient advisory board?

An advisory board is a small group who meet a few times a year. Our members let you ask many people with the condition whenever you have a question. Many teams use both: our members for breadth, the board for ongoing advice. Members can also join your advisory board as paid contributors.

How do you decide which members take part?

We start from the people your clinical trial needs, including the groups the evidence shows its clinical trials under-represent, by ethnicity, deprivation, age and language, and report the breakdown of everyone who took part. The breakdown for your condition near your sites comes with free patient feasibility.

Is patient feedback on our material confidential?

Yes. Members sign a confidentiality agreement when they join, and can sign your own wording too. They see your material inside our platform, where download and browser screenshots are blocked, and their confidentiality agreement covers photographing the screen. Material is taken down when the work ends.

Can patients help with recruitment strategy and site selection?

Yes. We show you where people with the condition live, from the first part of their postcodes, and ask them what would make taking part possible, such as travel, time off work and childcare. We report what they said. We don't promise it will change your enrolment.

Patient experience data

What is patient experience data?

What people tell us about living with a condition, their care and clinical trials, in their own words. The European Medicines Agency describes patient experience data as data that directly reflect the patient experience, without doctors or other parties interpreting them. We collect it from the same people over time, so findings can be compared, and every answer is broken down by who gave it.

How does your AI work, and can I check it?

Our AI is built only on what our members have told us: no synthetic patients and no outside data filling the gaps. Every answer links to the members' own answers it came from, and shows who gave them, so you can check it rather than trust it. It runs under Footnote, our own framework that sets what it may and may not do. It works in two steps. Ask our data: an instant answer from what members have already told us, broken down by who said it. Ask members live: one click sends the question to members whose profile fits, and answers come back in hours or days. Names and anything that could identify a member are removed before any text is analysed, and we never license anyone's answers to train AI.

How many patients like yours have already said yes?

Find out with free patient feasibility. Tell us the condition and where your sites are, and we'll reply the same working day with how many members near them match and have opted in to hear about clinical trials, and how they break down.
