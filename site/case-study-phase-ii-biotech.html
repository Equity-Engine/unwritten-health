Case study · Our pilot · phase 2

A phase 2 clinical trial that needed 500 people of African descent in 12 weeks.

A small-to-midsize biotech was preparing a phase 2 clinical trial for a mutation found only in people of African heritage. We were brought in before the protocol was final. People living with the condition reshaped the eligibility criteria, the visit schedule, the site strategy and the consent materials, and the programme went on to enrol 560 people in the 12-week window, 112% of target.

Clinical trial: phase 2 · Sponsor: small-to-midsize biotech · Brought in: before the protocol was final · The sponsor can't be named.

The brief: 500 people of African descent, in 12 weeks

The clinical trial centred on a mutation found only in people of African heritage. Reaching the right people wasn't a box to tick: without them, the clinical trial couldn't produce valid data. The brief was clear and unforgiving: 500 participants of African descent living with the condition, within a 12-week window.

For clinical accuracy, we partnered with a genetic testing laboratory, so eligibility rested on the test, not on self-report.

Why before the protocol was final

The most important thing about what followed was the timing. Once a protocol is final, the design is fixed and changes get expensive. Criteria that rule out more people than the science requires shrink the pool, slow recruitment and raise the risk of amending the protocol later.

57%

In two Tufts CSDD studies of multinational commercial clinical trials, 57% and 58.8% had submitted at least one amendment. The most common was a change to the trial population, including the eligibility criteria.

Getz et al., as summarised in Amendments Assemble, Trials, 2023.

What changed before the protocol was final

Each change came from what people living with the condition told us about how they live, their health and what would stop them taking part: the things a standard feasibility exercise doesn't surface.

1

The eligibility criteria

Each criterion was checked against how people in the communities this clinical trial needed actually live. Criteria that look neutral in a protocol can rule out whole groups in practice: age cut-offs that exclude older adults, laboratory thresholds that don't allow for conditions more common in some ethnic groups, and exclusions for other conditions that reflect the health of wealthier patients. Criteria that would have ruled out more people than the science required were flagged, and the protocol was refined before they were locked in.

2

The visit schedule

The visits were mapped against real lives. For people in part-time or shift work, frequent clinic visits mean lost income. For carers, visits without flexible windows are a barrier in themselves. Where the schedule risked losing people it didn't need to, changes were proposed before the design was fixed.

3

The site strategy

The original plan leaned on large academic medical centre sites: the default for most sponsors, but often the sites the people this clinical trial needed find hardest to reach, trust least and live furthest from. They are also slower to open.

8.12 vs 4.37 months

Median site activation time, from site selection to a completed contract: academic medical centres and hospitals against independent sites and physician practices, in phase 1 to 3 clinical trials.

WCG Clinical, Decoding the Top Site Challenges of 2024: Study Start-Up (WCG data, 2023).

The site strategy was revised to add community sites alongside the academic sites, based on where people living with the condition are, not corrected after recruitment had stalled.

4

The consent materials

People living with the condition read the participant information and consent materials before they were final, and the materials were changed to close the gaps in understanding they found, before the ethics submission rather than during review.

The outcome

The programme enrolled 560 people within the 12-week window, 12% over the 500 target, with eligibility confirmed through the genetic testing partnership. Alongside recruitment, start-up is projected at six months: two to four months earlier than it would otherwise have started.

The sponsor confirmed that the three things the work was set up to do were all done:

  • The voices of people living with the condition were in the protocol before lock.
  • Recruitment was delivered within the agreed 12-week window.
  • Less back-and-forth with regulators, with gaps in representation and understanding dealt with up front rather than raised in review.
Estimate · projected, not a result

What two to four months is worth

If the projected start-up holds, here is what the time is worth at Tufts CSDD's 2024 figures.

$1.4M
in direct running costs over 60 days, at $23,737 a day for a phase 2 clinical trial
$2.8M
in direct running costs over 120 days, at the same rate
$800,000
in lost prescription sales for each day a medicine reaches the market later

Tufts CSDD, Quantifying the Value of a Day of Delay in Drug Development (Smith, DiMasi and Getz), August 2024. Direct costs only: the $1.4M to $2.8M doesn't include the value of reaching the market earlier. Every study is different.

What they did, and what they didn't

This was our pilot, done by hand, before the platform. The input from people living with the condition was gathered for the pilot specifically.

Their work was the eligibility criteria, the visit schedule, the site strategy and the consent materials, not the enrolment. The enrolment is the sponsor's, and many things went into it. We don't claim their work caused it, and we don't promise it for anyone else.

The thinking behind this approach is set out in our white paper, Health equity risk in clinical research: how to see patient recruitment failure before it happens, free on our Resources page.
